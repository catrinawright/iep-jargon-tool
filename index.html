👨‍👩‍👧 Scenarios for Parents and Guardians

  • Before the MeetingYou receive a draft IEP and do not recognize terms like PLAAFP, SDI, or LRE. Use the Glossary tab to look up each term before you walk in the door.
  • During the MeetingThe team proposes a goal but it sounds like what your child already does. Use the Endrew F. tab to understand the "appropriately ambitious" standard and speak up.
  • You Disagree with a DecisionThe school says your child does not qualify for extended school year services. Use the Action Scripts tab — specifically "I disagree with a goal" and "Requesting an independent evaluation" — to frame your response.
  • You Feel RushedThe team pushes to finish quickly and sign the IEP. Use the script: "I am not ready to sign today. I would like to take the document home and review it."
  • Services Are Not HappeningYour child's speech therapy has not started weeks after the IEP was signed. Use the script for "The school is not implementing the IEP."
You are an equal member of the IEP team. You are not a guest. You are not required to sign on the day of the meeting. Your signature means you attended — not that you agree.

🎓 Scenarios for Students

  • Your First IEP MeetingYou are invited to your IEP meeting but do not know what to say. Start with the Student Guide tab. Read each term and practice saying what you want out loud before the meeting.
  • You Want to Change a GoalYour goal feels too easy or not connected to what you actually want to do after school. You can say: "I would like to talk about changing this goal because it is not what I am working toward."
  • You Do Not Understand SomethingA teacher or staff member uses a word you do not recognize in your meeting. You can say: "Can you explain what that means in a simpler way?"
  • Transition PlanningYour team asks what you want to do after high school. Use the Transition Plan card in the Student Guide to prepare your answer before the meeting.
  • You Want More IndependenceYou feel like adults are making decisions for you without asking. Self-determination is your right. You can say: "I would like to lead part of this meeting and share my own goals."
You have the legal right to be at your own IEP meeting. Your voice matters more than anyone else's in that room. The Student Guide tab was built for you.

📚 Scenarios for Teachers

  • Family Cannot Follow the MeetingA parent looks confused but does not ask questions. Pause and say: "I want to make sure this is clear. Can I explain that in a different way?" Use this tool as a shared reference point on screen or printed.
  • Writing Measurable GoalsA goal is not specific enough to measure. Review the Annual Goals definition and the Endrew F. standard to ensure goals reflect meaningful growth beyond current performance.
  • Explaining SDI to a FamilyA parent asks why their child is being pulled from the classroom. Use the Specially Designed Instruction and LRE definitions to explain the decision in plain language.
  • Student is Not Making ProgressYou need to initiate a conversation about revising goals mid-year. Use the Prior Written Notice and IEP amendment script to frame the process correctly.
  • Introducing a New Family to IEP ProcessA family is attending their first IEP meeting. Share the QR code for this tool before the meeting so they can review terms in advance.
Sharing this tool with families before the meeting — not during — gives them time to build vocabulary and arrive as informed participants rather than passive recipients.

🏫 Scenarios for Administrators

  • Compliance DisputeA family alleges FAPE was denied because services were not implemented as written. Review the FAPE and Procedural Safeguards definitions to ensure your team can articulate the legal standard clearly.
  • Parent Requests Due ProcessA parent files for due process. Review the Procedural Safeguards section and the Endrew F. standard — both will be central to the hearing.
  • Staff Training GapsTeachers are writing goals that reflect current performance rather than meaningful growth. Use this tool as a staff resource during IEP professional development, alongside the Endrew F. page.
  • Transition ComplianceA student approaching age 16 does not have a transition plan in their IEP. Review the Transition Plan and Postsecondary Goals definitions to ensure legal requirements are met.
  • Family Engagement BarriersFamilies attend meetings but do not participate. Share the QR code for this tool in your IEP meeting invitation letters as a preparation resource.
Under Endrew F. (2017), "more than de minimis" is no longer a sufficient legal standard. Programs must be appropriately ambitious. This applies to every student, at every level, in every placement.

⚖️ Scenarios for Advocates

  • Preparing a Family Before a MeetingUse the Glossary and Action Scripts tabs to walk a family through the key terms and phrases they will need. Focus on FAPE, LRE, Prior Written Notice, and Endrew F.
  • Disputing an Inadequate IEPGoals reflect current performance with no expectation of growth. Cite the Endrew F. standard directly: programs must be appropriately ambitious. Document the specific goals that fail to meet this standard.
  • Requesting an IEEThe school's evaluation does not reflect the student's actual profile. Use the script for "Requesting an independent evaluation" and submit the request in writing.
  • Parent Was Not Meaningfully InformedA parent signed an IEP without understanding it. Review the Procedural Safeguards section — informed consent requires that the parent genuinely understood what they were agreeing to.
  • Placement Is More Restrictive Than NecessaryA student is in a separate classroom with no general education exposure. Use the LRE definition and the Endrew F. standard to frame the argument for a less restrictive placement.
Prior Written Notice is one of the most underused procedural tools available to families. Every time a school refuses to act on a parent's request, a PWN is required. No PWN means no documented basis for the refusal — that is a procedural violation.
